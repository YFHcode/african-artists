## The World

By 1976 Sam Nzima was a photographer for *The World*, a Johannesburg newspaper with a large Black readership, particularly in Soweto. It covered the township's life and its politics, at a time when most of the white press ignored both.

## 16 June 1976

On the morning of 16 June 1976, thousands of Soweto schoolchildren marched against a government order that half of their subjects be taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire. Nzima saw a young man, Mbuyisa Makhubo, running with the body of a schoolboy in his arms, the boy's sister, Antoinette Sithole, running beside them, her hand raised and her face open in grief. The boy was Hector Pieterson. He died soon afterwards.

Nzima took a short sequence of pictures. *The World* published one of them that afternoon, and within days it had been reproduced around the world. It turned the Soweto uprising — and the apartheid state's response to children — into a story no government could explain away.

## The cost

The photograph made Nzima a target. Harassed by the security police, he went into hiding and then returned to his home area in what is now Mpumalanga, where he was watched and, by some accounts, kept under house arrest. When *The World* was closed by the government, other newspapers offered him work; he declined, fearing for his safety. In 1979 he was appointed to the legislative assembly of Gazankulu, one of the apartheid-era homelands.

For many years Nzima had no control over his own photograph. The rights were returned to him only in the late 1990s, after a long effort.

## Memory

The Hector Pieterson Memorial and Museum, opened in Soweto in 2002, stands a short walk from where the picture was taken, and the image is part of it. 16 June is now a public holiday in South Africa, Youth Day. Nzima died in 2018, aged 83.
