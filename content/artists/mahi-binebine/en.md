## From mathematics to art

Mahi Binebine was born in Marrakech in 1959. In 1980 he went to Paris to study mathematics, and for about eight years he taught the subject there. He painted and wrote in his spare time until art took over: by the late 1980s he was exhibiting as a painter, and in 1992 he published his first novel.

He lived in Madrid and then in New York from 1994 to 1999, where his work entered the collection of the Guggenheim Museum, before returning to Marrakech in 2002.

## The paintings and sculptures

Binebine's paintings are crowded with figures: bodies pressed together, faces hidden behind masks, people bound, packed into boats or waiting. He works with pigments, wax and resin on canvas and wood, often in earthy colours with sudden accents of red or blue. He also makes sculptures, many of them in bronze, which repeat the same anonymous, mask-like heads.

Imprisonment and confinement run through the work, and they have a family history. In 1971 his elder brother Aziz, a young army officer, was caught up in a failed coup against King Hassan II. He spent eighteen years in the secret desert prison of Tazmamart, where half the prisoners died; his testimony inspired [Tahar Ben Jelloun](/artists/tahar-ben-jelloun)'s novel *This Blinding Absence of Light*. Their father, meanwhile, was a companion and jester at the king's court — a story Mahi told in his novel *Le Fou du roi* (2017).

## The novels

Binebine has published more than a dozen novels in French. *Cannibales* (1999; English: *Welcome to Paradise*) follows migrants waiting to cross the Strait of Gibraltar. *Les Étoiles de Sidi Moumen* (2010) imagines the lives of the young men from a Casablanca slum who carried out the suicide bombings of 16 May 2003. Translated as *Horses of God*, it was adapted by the director Nabil Ayouch into a film of the same name, Morocco's entry for the Oscars.

With Ayouch he then opened a cultural centre in Sidi Moumen itself, Les Étoiles, where young people from the district learn music, dance, film and art.
