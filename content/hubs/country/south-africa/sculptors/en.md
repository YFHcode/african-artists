South African sculpture has long been bound up with power and memory. In the first half of the twentieth century, sculptors such as Anton van Wouw and Coert Steynberg made the bronze presidents, generals and pioneers that filled the country's squares and monuments, many of them celebrating the Afrikaner story that apartheid was built on. Ernest Mancoba's carved wooden figures of the 1920s and 1930s, and the work of artists trained at centres such as Polly Street and Rorke's Drift, offered another tradition.

From the 1980s sculpture became one of the strongest voices of resistance and reckoning. Jane Alexander's *Butcher Boys* (1985–86), three seated figures with animal heads, remains one of the most disturbing images of the apartheid era. Since 1994, artists such as Mary Sibande, with her life-size figures of a domestic worker named Sophie, and Nandipha Mntambo, who works with cowhide, have brought new subjects to the medium. These are the country's sculptors, most looked-up first. See also [South African artists](/artists/country/south-africa) and [African sculpture](/art-forms/african-sculpture).
