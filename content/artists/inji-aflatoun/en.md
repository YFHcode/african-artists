## A rebel from a wealthy family

Inji Aflatoun was born in Cairo in 1924 into an aristocratic family and educated at the French Lycée. As a teenager she was taught by the painter Kamel El-Telmissany, one of the founders of the Egyptian surrealist group Art and Freedom (*al-Fann wa al-Hurriyya*), and she exhibited with the group in the early 1940s. Her early paintings are dark and dreamlike.

Through the same circles she discovered Marxism. She became a communist and a feminist, spoke for Egyptian women at international congresses after the Second World War, and wrote pamphlets on women's rights and national independence.

## Painting the people

From the late 1940s her painting turned to the real lives of Egyptians: peasant women in the fields, workers, fishermen and their families, painted in a direct, socially committed style. She travelled in the countryside to paint, and her work became part of her politics.

## Prison

In 1959 President Nasser's government arrested hundreds of communists. Aflatoun was held from June 1959 to July 1963, most of that time in the women's prison at Qanater, north of Cairo. Painting was forbidden at first, but she persuaded the authorities to let her work, and for the first years she painted the women around her: political prisoners and ordinary criminals, sleeping in the crowded dormitory, waiting, working, looking out through the bars. Later she painted what she could see beyond the walls — trees and flowers seen through bars.

These prison paintings, quiet and compassionate rather than angry, are now regarded as the high point of her work and among the most important images in modern Egyptian art.

## Light

After her release her style changed. Her landscapes of the Nile, the desert and the countryside are made of short, separated strokes of colour on a bare white ground, so that the canvas seems filled with light. She exhibited in Egypt, Europe and the socialist countries, and died in Cairo in 1989.
