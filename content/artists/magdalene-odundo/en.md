## From Nairobi to Farnham

Magdalene Anyango Namakhiya Odundo was born in Nairobi in 1950 and spent part of her childhood in India. She trained first in commercial art in Nairobi, and in 1971 moved to England, where a visit to a pottery studio changed her direction. She studied ceramics at the West Surrey College of Art and Design in Farnham and later took a master's degree at the Royal College of Art in London, completed in 1982.

## Learning from women potters

A decisive moment came in 1974, when she travelled to the Pottery Training Centre in Abuja, Nigeria, founded by Michael Cardew, where [Ladi Kwali](/artists/ladi-kwali) had made her name. There she learned the hand-building methods of Gbari women potters, and she later studied pottery traditions in Kenya as well. Those African techniques — coiling, scraping, burnishing with stones, open firing — became the basis of her art, combined with what she saw in ancient ceramics from Egypt, Greece, the Americas and Asia.

## The vessels

Odundo does not use a wheel. Each vessel is built slowly by hand, coated with a fine clay slip and polished with stones until it shines. She then fires it, and for the black pieces fires it again in a reduced-oxygen kiln so that carbon turns the surface black; the result looks almost like metal or stone. The forms are her own: tall necks that lean to one side, swelling bellies, lugs and spouts that suggest arms, ears or knots of hair. They can be read as bodies, as heads, or simply as pots, and they hold a perfect, quiet balance.

## Recognition

Her work is in more than fifty museums, including the British Museum, the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Smithsonian and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. In 2019 the Sainsbury Centre in Norwich gave her a major exhibition, *The Journey of Things*, setting her vessels alongside objects from many cultures that inspired her. She taught for many years and became Chancellor of the University for the Creative Arts in 2018, and in 2020 she was made a Dame.
