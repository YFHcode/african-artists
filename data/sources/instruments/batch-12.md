## kalimba
- Hugh Tracey (1903-1977), British-born ethnomusicologist; founded the International Library of African Music in 1954 (moved to Rhodes University, Grahamstown, by his son Andrew in 1978) and, in 1954, the African Musical Instruments company (AMI), which made the original "Hugh Tracey Kalimba" (musicinafrica.net https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/3552 ; Rhodes University https://www.ru.ac.za/latestnews/archives/2009/2009-11-051731.html ; Boston University library guide https://library.bu.edu/AfricanMusic/ArchivesRepositories).
- Modelled on the Shona mbira nyunga nyunga; name "kalimba" from an older term for the family (kalimbamagic.com https://www.kalimbamagic.com/info/history/hugh-tracey-meets-the-african-thumb-piano ; larkinthemorning.com https://larkinthemorning.com/blogs/articles/kalimbas-sansulas-history). Retail and enthusiast sites: treated as indicative, not authoritative.
- Tine count: sources disagree. One says 15 tines tuned to G major for Tracey's own; others a 17-tine "treble" model, which most modern kalimbas follow. The page says so and does not choose.
- Earth, Wind & Fire "Kalimba Story": the page says only "in the 1970s" (a source gave 1973, which is doubtful; the song is from their 1974 album, not checked).
- 1586, João dos Santos, "ambira" among the Shona (Horniman Museum record HC.2017.151 ; uark.pressbooks.pub "Shona bira" ; Grinnell Musical Instruments item).
- NOT used: Zambian lamellae of 500-700 CE (one source only, uncertain); lamellophone names beyond mbira, sanza, likembe (general knowledge, widely attested; no source fetched).
- Standard layout (longest tine in the centre, higher notes alternating outward), C major as the common tuning, numbered tabs, the tuning-hammer note: common instruction in beginner guides, not independently sourced here; marked in the page as "common".

## djembe
- Mande origin, Mali Empire region; links to the numu blacksmith caste; no written records, dating uncertain (drumconnection.com https://www.drumconnection.com/africa-connections/history-of-the-djembe ; en.wikipedia Djembe https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djembe in search snippets ; wmic.net).
- "anke djé, anke bé" (Bamana), "gather together in peace": popular etymology, disputed; the page calls it tradition.
- Hardwood body, usually goatskin; traditional carvers in Mali, Guinea, Burkina Faso and Senegal; exported instruments may use substitute woods, machine carving, synthetic heads, fibreglass or mechanical tuning (soundbridge.io https://www.soundbridge.io/djembe).
- Les Ballets Africains: founded by Fodéba Keïta; founding date given variously as 1948 (Paris, ensemble), 1950 and 1952 (en.wikipedia Les Ballets Africains ; numeridanse.com https://numeridanse.com/en/profile/ballets-africains ; africultures.com). The page says "between 1948 and 1952". National ensemble after Guinea's independence (1958); other countries followed with national ballets in the 1960s.
- Famoudou Konaté principal djembe soloist 1959-1985; after Sékou Touré's death in 1984 funding dried up and djembefolas such as Mamady Keïta (1950-2021) emigrated and taught (worldmusiccentral.org ; wesleyan guide to the jembe https://echarry.faculty.wesleyan.edu/?p=553).
- Three sounds (bass, tone, slap), dunun accompaniment, the lead drummer calling breaks: standard teaching description; no single source fetched in this pass.

## mbira
- Mbira dzavadzimu of the Zezuru Shona, "iron mbira" (nhare); 22 to 28 keys on a hardwood board; deze gourd resonator; buzzers of bottle tops, beads or shells; fibreglass replacing gourds because of drought; bira ceremony; kushaura and kutsinhira; hosho (Grinnell https://omeka-s.grinnell.edu/s/MusicalInstruments/item/1828 ; Horniman ; uark.pressbooks.pub https://uark.pressbooks.pub/musicinworldcultures/chapter/shona-bira/ ; sympathetic-resonances.org https://sympathetic-resonances.org/help/mbira).
- Paul Berliner's work of the 1970s brought it to an international audience (same sources).
- 1586 João dos Santos (above). "More than 1000 years": one source claims it; the page says it is hard to verify.
- UNESCO: "Art of crafting and playing Mbira/Sansi, the finger-plucking traditional musical instrument in Malawi and Zimbabwe", Representative List, 2020 (15.COM 8.B.38), reference 01541; not inscribed in 2018 (https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/01541 ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/15.COM/8.B.38).
- NOT used: the claim that Thomas Mapfumo or other chimurenga musicians adapted mbira to guitar (well known, but no source fetched in this pass); named tunings Nyamaropa and Mavembe (seller description only).

## kora
- 21 strings; calabash body with cowhide; hide or gut strings once, nylon fishing line now; plucked with thumbs and index fingers (whro.org https://www.whro.org/2024-07-29/toumani-diabate-plucked-the-koras-21-strings-the-world-fell-in-love-with-his-music ; africa-press.net https://www.africa-press.net/gambia/?p=5672).
- Jeli families: Kouyaté, Jobarteh/Diabaté, Cissoko/Sissoko (superscout.co/innovation/kora ; africa-press.net).
- Origin accounts differ (Mali Empire; kingdom of Kaabu; calabash harps in the Mandinka lands); oral tradition; the page says the origin is unrecorded.
- Toumani Diabaté (1965-2024): 71st generation in his family's line; In the Heart of the Moon with Ali Farka Touré won the Grammy for Traditional World Music Album at the 2006 ceremony (grammy.com https://www.grammy.com/awards/categories/traditional-world-music-album/2006/); New Ancient Strings with Ballaké Sissoko (an homage to Ancient Strings); died 19 July 2024 aged 58 (NPR https://www.npr.org/sections/goats-and-soda/2024/07/29/g-s1-13359/ ; VOA https://www.voaafrica.com/a/7745186.html ; WHRO). Son Sidiki Diabaté (worldmusiccentral.org).
- NOT used: who made the original Ancient Strings (1970) — worded "an older generation".

## balafon
- Sosso-Bala, Niagassola, northern Guinea; described as 13th century, about 1.5 m long, 20 slats, several gourds per slat; legend: made by the king or given by a jinni; guardian the balatigui of the Dökala family; proclaimed a Masterpiece in 2001, Representative List 2008; symbol of the freedom and cohesion of the Mandinka community; Sunjata epic; threat: rural exodus, fewer pupils (UNESCO https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00009 ; superscout.co/innovation/sosso-bala). One folk-festival page says "about 1,000 years old" - not used.
- Balla Kouyaté, National Heritage Fellow of the US National Endowment for the Arts (https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/balla-kouyate).
- Mande name "bala"; gourd resonators; geography Mali/Guinea/Burkina Faso: general description; no single source fetched.
- NOT used: mirliton membranes; playing technique in pairs.

## talking drum
- Hourglass, two heads joined by leather cords, pitch changed by squeezing (Frontiers for Young Minds https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2022.755390/pdf ; Smithsonian Magazine https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/how-west-african-drums-really-talk-180978296/).
- Yoruba: three tones (H, M, L); lead drum ìyá ìlù; 2021 Frontiers in Communication study of 30 spoken and sung Yoruba recordings against dùndún drumming by professional Nigerian drummers; parallel intensity and timing patterns; accuracy lower in purely musical playing; uses: announcements, warnings, prayers, jokes, proverbs, poetry (https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2021.650382/full ; PRI ; Scimex). Pitch, volume and rhythm but not vowels and consonants (Wikipedia Talking drum, via search snippet).
- Tama (Wolof, Senegal): existing mbalax page; held under the arm, curved stick.
- Dagomba of northern Ghana: a sophisticated griot tradition centred on the talking drum (Wikipedia, snippet). "Lunsi" and "Lunna/Donno" spellings could not be confirmed - not used.
- NOT used: Fela Kuti, King Sunny Adé and other popular users (nothing found).
