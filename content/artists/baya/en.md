## A childhood and a discovery

Baya was born Fatma Haddad in 1931 in Bordj El Kiffan, east of Algiers. She lost both parents young and as a girl went to work in the household of Marguerite Caminat Benhoura, a Frenchwoman in Algiers who noticed her modelling clay figures and painting, and gave her materials and space.

In 1947 the Paris dealer Aimé Maeght saw her work and gave her a solo exhibition at his gallery. André Breton, the leader of Surrealism, wrote the catalogue preface. Baya had just turned sixteen. Maeght's magazine *Derrière le Miroir* devoted its sixth issue, dated November 1947, to her, with a text by Breton, so her work reached the Paris art world as a publication as well as a show.

## Vallauris

In 1948 she worked at the Madoura pottery in Vallauris, in the south of France, where Pablo Picasso was making ceramics at the same time. The two met there, and many writers have traced an exchange between Baya's work and Picasso's paintings of Algerian women from the following years. A 2013 exhibition at Vallauris brought together some thirty of her early gouaches and ceramics, drawn from the holdings of the Maeght Foundation.

## Women, birds and gardens

Baya's world is one of women in long patterned dresses, surrounded by birds, fish, butterflies, plants, fruit and musical instruments — lutes, darboukas — in saturated blues, reds, pinks and greens. There is no perspective and no shadow. Figures and ornament share the same flat, rhythmic surface, and men are almost entirely absent.

Critics in the 1940s called her a "naive" or Surrealist painter. She accepted neither label. Today the work is read as an independent, deeply Algerian vision: an imagined interior world painted with total confidence.

## How to look at a Baya

Start with the surface. The gouaches are painted on paper in flat, opaque colour, so nothing recedes: a woman's dress, a bird on her hand and a lute beside her all sit on the same plane and compete for your eye on equal terms. Follow the patterns. The borders of the dresses, the feathers and the strings of the instruments repeat a few shapes in different colours, which gives the pictures their rhythm. Then notice what is missing: perspective, shadow and, almost always, men. The ceramics she made in Vallauris, such as a reclining woman in enamelled terracotta, put the same women into clay. Seen this way, the work reads less as naive than as composed, the product of a painter who knew exactly how much she wanted to leave out.

## Silence and return

In 1953 Baya married the musician El Hadj Mahfoud Mahieddine and largely stopped painting for about a decade, raising a family in Blida during the Algerian War of Independence. She returned to painting in 1963, after independence. That year she showed at the National Museum of Fine Arts in Algiers, encouraged by its director Jean de Maisonseul, and in 1964 she exhibited in Paris. She went on showing in Algeria and abroad until her death in Blida in 1998.

## Rediscovery

Recent exhibitions have brought her work back into view, including *Baya: Woman of Algiers* at New York University's Grey Art Gallery in 2018 and her inclusion in the main exhibition of the 2022 Venice Biennale, *The Milk of Dreams*. The Sharjah Art Museum mounted *Lasting Impressions: Baya Mahieddine* in 2021, and the Institut du monde arabe in Paris showed her work from November 2022 to March 2023 in its exhibition on Algeria. In December 2022 Qatar National Library, with Mathaf, put on eighteen of her paintings from Qatari collections. These are the museums to check first if you want to see her work in person.

## Related artists and music
