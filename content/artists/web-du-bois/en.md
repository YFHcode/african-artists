Du Bois is known above all for his writing and activism — *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), his decades at the head of the civil-rights movement, and his role in Pan-Africanism. Wikidata lists him as a photographer, and he did not take photographs professionally. But he understood earlier than almost anyone what photographs could argue.

For the 1900 Exposition Universelle in Paris, Du Bois and Thomas J. Calloway assembled the *Exhibit of American Negroes*: hundreds of photographs of Black Americans — portraits of students, professionals and families, homes, schools and businesses — shown with hand-drawn charts of Black life in Georgia. Many of the photographs were made by Black photographers such as Thomas E. Askew of Atlanta. Against the caricatures of the time, the exhibit presented Black Americans as they saw themselves. The photographs are now held by the Library of Congress.

In 1961, at ninety-three, Du Bois moved to Ghana at the invitation of President Kwame Nkrumah to work on an *Encyclopedia Africana*. He became a Ghanaian citizen and died in Accra on 27 August 1963, the eve of the March on Washington. His home there is now a memorial centre.
