Forty-two artists from fifteen countries whose work changed how the world sees art from Africa: painters, sculptors and photographers, from the 1920s to today. Fourteen have full profiles, listed below with their life, key dates and where the work can be seen. The other twenty-eight are introduced here, grouped by role, with links wherever we have more.

The selection spans the continent and its languages on purpose: Anglophone West and East Africa, Francophone Mali, Senegal and the Congo, Lusophone Mozambique, Arabic-speaking Egypt, Sudan, Algeria and Morocco, and Southern Africa. For the schools these artists formed or rebelled against, see [art movements](/movements); for women artists beyond these profiles, see our guide to [African women artists](/guides/african-women-artists).

## Pioneers and the schools

**Mahmoud Mokhtar** (Egypt, 1891–1934) made *Egypt's Awakening*, a monumental sculpture in Cairo that became a national emblem of the 1920s.

**Gerard Sekoto** (South Africa, 1913–1993) painted township life, left for Paris in 1947 and became a founding figure of Black South African modernism.

**Inji Efflatoun** (Egypt, 1924–1989) painted her fellow prisoners from her own cell, joining art, politics and feminism. See [African women artists](/guides/african-women-artists).

**Chaïbia Talal** (Morocco) was a self-taught painter whose dense, vivid canvases are among the best known of the [Casablanca School](/movements/casablanca-school).

**Mohamed Melehi** (Morocco) helped found the Casablanca School with [Farid Belkahia](/artists/farid-belkahia), and is known for bold, rhythmic wave patterns in strong colour.

**Uche Okeke** (Nigeria) helped shape the [Zaria Art Society](/movements/zaria-art-society) and its idea of "natural synthesis", the argument that modern Nigerian art should draw on its own traditions.

**Bruce Onobrakpeya** (Nigeria) is a printmaker and painter, also linked to the Zaria Art Society, whose work draws on the textures and stories of Nigeria's many peoples.

**Bodys Isek Kingelez** (DR Congo, 1948–2015) built fantastical model cities from paper, cardboard and found materials, imagining a Kinshasa of the future.

## Sculptors and installation artists

**[Romuald Hazoumè](/artists/romuald-hazoume)** (Benin) makes masks from discarded petrol cans, many of them referring to fuel smuggling and to the Atlantic slave trade. See [African masks](/art-forms/african-masks).

**Yinka Shonibare MBE** (Nigeria and UK) uses Dutch wax print, a fabric sold across Africa as "African" but made in Europe, to dress headless figures in Victorian costume.

**Serge Attukwei Clottey** (Ghana) turns yellow plastic jerrycans into woven wall pieces and sculptures, a practice he calls Afrogallonism.

**Ibrahim Mahama** (Ghana) wraps buildings, monuments and railway wagons in jute sacks, tracing the trade that moves through them. See [African textiles](/art-forms/african-textiles).

**Gonçalo Mabunda** (Mozambique) makes thrones, masks and sculptures from decommissioned weapons left over from the civil war. See [African sculpture](/art-forms/african-sculpture).

**Nicholas Hlobo** (South Africa) stitches rubber inner tubes and laces them with ribbon, drawing on Xhosa ceremony and on questions of identity.

**Meschac Gaba** (Benin) is best known for his *Museum of Contemporary African Art*, an installation of rooms built from cloth, games and everyday objects.

**Abdoulaye Konaté** (Mali) makes large textile works woven from strips of cloth, often addressing social and political questions. See [African textiles](/art-forms/african-textiles).

**Barthélémy Toguo** (Cameroon) makes installations, drawings and prints about migration, globalisation and the body, working between Cameroon and Europe.

## Painters

**William Kentridge** (South Africa) draws in charcoal, erases and redraws each image, and films the results, from *Felix in Exile* onward. See [contemporary African art](/guides/contemporary-african-art).

**Marlene Dumas** (South Africa, based in Amsterdam) paints portraits from photographs, with a haunting, unfinished intimacy.

**Julie Mehretu** (Ethiopia, based in the United States) builds large, layered drawings and paintings of cities, maps and architecture.

**Michael Armitage** (Kenya) paints large canvases that layer Kenyan folklore, history and colonial-era imagery.

**Amoako Boafo** (Ghana) paints portraits of friends and strangers in bold colour and expressive, sculptural brushwork.

## Photographers and visual artists

**Samuel Fosso** (Cameroon) makes staged self-portraits in costume that play with identity, fashion and stereotype. See [African photography](/art-forms/african-photography).

**Lalla Essaydi** (Morocco) photographs women in embroidered fabric covered in henna calligraphy, challenging stereotypes of Arab women.

**Hassan Hajjaj** (Morocco and UK) frames colourful portraits with Moroccan fabrics, tins and everyday objects, linking Marrakech and London.

**Youssef Nabil** (Egypt) hand-tints photographic portraits in the manner of early cinema and studio portraiture.

**Sammy Baloji** (DR Congo) makes photographs and films about Katanga's mining history and colonial archives. See [African photography](/art-forms/african-photography).

**Zanele Muholi** (South Africa) is a visual activist whose portraits of Black LGBTQIA+ South Africans, in series such as *Faces and Phases*, have reached museums worldwide.
