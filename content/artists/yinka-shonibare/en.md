## Between London and Lagos

Yinka Shonibare was born in London in 1962 and grew up in Lagos, speaking Yoruba at home and English at school. At eighteen, back in London to study art, he contracted transverse myelitis, an inflammation of the spinal cord that left him partly paralysed; he has worked with a team of assistants ever since. He studied at the Byam Shaw School of Art and Goldsmiths College.

## The fabric of empire

As a student he was asked why he did not make "authentic African art". His answer became his signature material: Dutch wax print, the brightly patterned cotton worn across West Africa and seen in Europe as typically African. Its history is anything but simple: it was developed in the Netherlands, inspired by Indonesian batik, made for colonial markets, and later adopted and reinvented in West Africa. For Shonibare it stands for the tangled, hybrid identities that colonialism produced.

He dresses life-size, headless mannequins in Victorian and eighteenth-century costume tailored from this fabric — aristocrats at play, explorers, a duel — so that the clothes of European power are made from the cloth of its colonies. *Scramble for Africa* (2003) shows the European leaders of the 1884 Berlin Conference, headless, carving up a map of the continent.

## Public works

In 2010 *Nelson's Ship in a Bottle*, a scale model of HMS Victory with sails of wax print, was shown on the Fourth Plinth in Trafalgar Square; it is now at the National Maritime Museum. *The British Library* (2014) lines shelves with thousands of books bound in wax print, their spines printed with the names of immigrants to Britain and of those who opposed immigration.

## Recognition and foundations

Shonibare was nominated for the Turner Prize in 2004, elected a Royal Academician in 2013 and appointed CBE in 2019. He runs Guest Projects, a space for other artists in his London studio, and founded the G.A.S. Foundation, with residencies in Lagos and on a farm at Ijebu, in Nigeria.
