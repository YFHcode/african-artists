## Born in the townships

Amapiano began as an experimental sound around 2012, passed around in long DJ mixes on USB drives and phones, played in minibus taxis, street parties and township taverns. Its exact birthplace is argued over: some place it in Johannesburg townships such as Soweto and Alexandra, others in Pretoria, where it overlaps with the earlier Bacardi house sound. The name is credited to the duo MFR Souls.

It drew on South Africa's long line of dance music — kwaito, deep house, jazz and gqom — and slowed it down, leaving space for piano chords, vocal chants and long builds.

## The log drum

The sound that makes amapiano instantly recognisable is the **log drum**: a deep, pitched, percussive bass that thuds and slides under the beat. Kabza De Small credits the producer MDU aka TRP with discovering the sound while experimenting with software instruments. Once heard, it spread everywhere.

## Stars and spread

Kabza De Small and DJ Maphorisa, both from Pretoria, shaped the genre's rise; as the Scorpion Kings they released a stream of collaborations that took it from townships to the national mainstream. Focalistic's "Ke Star" became one of the first amapiano songs to chart internationally. DJs and performers such as Uncle Waffles took it onto festival stages abroad.

By the early 2020s amapiano was the dominant sound of Southern African clubs and was being absorbed by Nigerian [Afrobeats](/music/afrobeats) producers. In 2024 the South African singer Tyla won the first Grammy for Best African Music Performance with "Water", a pop hit built on amapiano rhythms.

## How to recognise it

- Tempo around 110–115 beats per minute, slower than most house music.
- The log drum bass, often playing off the beat.
- Jazz and soul piano chords, synth pads, shakers and percussion.
- Vocal hooks and chants in isiZulu, Sesotho, Setswana, English and township slang.
