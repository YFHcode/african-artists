## Cuban records on the Congo River

In the 1940s, records of Cuban son and rumba — played by radio stations and sold by traders — became hugely popular in the twin capitals of Léopoldville and Brazzaville. Congolese musicians heard in them something familiar: rhythms that had travelled from Central Africa to the Caribbean with enslaved people, now coming back. They began to play their own versions, with guitars, in Lingala.

In 1948 Wendo Kolosoy recorded "Marie-Louise", co-written with Henri Bowane, for the new Ngoma label in Léopoldville. It became a hit across the region and is often cited as the first great Congolese rumba record.

## The great orchestras

The 1950s and 1960s were the age of the big bands. Joseph Kabasele, "Le Grand Kallé", led African Jazz, whose "Indépendance Cha Cha" became the anthem of the Congo's independence in 1960. Franco Luambo Makiadi founded OK Jazz in 1956; renamed TPOK Jazz, it became the most famous band in Africa over three decades, with Franco's guitar at its heart. Tabu Ley Rochereau modernised the sound and took it onto international stages.

## Sebene and soukous

The most distinctive part of a rumba song is the **sebene**: the fast, looping guitar section, usually in the second half, where the dancing starts. Later generations sped it up and built entire songs around it, giving rise to soukous and then ndombolo, the high-energy dance styles of the 1980s and 1990s.

Papa Wemba, a star of the band Zaïko Langa Langa before founding Viva la Musica, became an international figure and an icon of the sapeurs, the elegantly dressed fans of Kinshasa. Koffi Olomide and many others carried the music into the twenty-first century.

## UNESCO recognition

In December 2021 UNESCO added Congolese rumba to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, following a joint application by the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the Republic of the Congo — a rare shared nomination by the two countries on either bank of the river.
