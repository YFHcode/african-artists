## A camera from Senegal

Seydou Keïta was born in Bamako around 1921 and trained as a carpenter. In 1935 an uncle returning from Senegal gave him a Kodak Brownie, and he taught himself to photograph. In 1948 he opened a studio in the family house in Bamako-Koura, behind the main prison, and within a few years it was the place to have your portrait made.

## How a Keïta portrait works

Keïta photographed people as they wanted to be seen. Sitters arrived in their best clothes; he added what was missing from a stock of props and outfits he kept at the studio — European suits, watches, pens, a radio, a scooter, flowers — and posed them, alone or in groups, against patterned fabric backdrops that he changed every few years.

The result is a portrait of a society as much as of individuals: a city of civil servants, traders, young couples and extended families, confident and modern, in the last years of French rule. He mostly used a large-format camera and made contact prints straight from the negatives, which gives the pictures their sharpness.

## State photographer

When Mali became independent in 1960, the new socialist government needed photographers. In 1962 it made Keïta its official photographer, and he closed his studio soon afterwards. He worked for the state until he retired in 1977.

## Discovery

For years his archive was known mainly in Bamako. In the 1990s exhibitions in Europe and the United States brought his portraits to a wide audience, and debates followed about who should print and sell them. In 2016 the Grand Palais in Paris presented the first broad retrospective of his work, with nearly three hundred photographs. Keïta died in Paris in 2001.
