## Music of the musseques

In colonial Luanda, the musseques — the sand-floored neighbourhoods around the Portuguese city — were home to most Angolans and to a rich musical life. Semba grew there from older Kimbundu songs and dances, and its name is linked to *massemba*, a dance step in which partners' bellies touch.

## Ngola Ritmos and the idea of Angola

In 1947 a group of young men including **Liceu Vieira Dias** formed **Ngola Ritmos** in Luanda. They played Angolan rhythms on the acoustic guitar alongside traditional instruments such as the **dikanza**, a notched scraper, and **ngoma** drums, and they sang in Kimbundu at a time when the colonial regime discouraged African languages and culture.

That choice was political. Ngola Ritmos and the groups that followed helped create a sense of *angolanidade* — Angolan identity — that fed the push for independence. Several musicians of this generation were persecuted by the Portuguese authorities for their links to the nationalist movement.

## The electric era

In the 1960s and 1970s, semba went electric. Bands such as Os Kiezos, and singers such as **Elias dya Kimuezo**, often called the "king of Angolan music", filled Luanda's dance halls with guitar-driven semba.

**Bonga** (José Adelino Barceló de Carvalho, born 1942), a former athlete who went into exile in Europe, became semba's best-known voice abroad. His 1972 album *Angola 72*, with songs such as "Mona Ki Ngi Xica", is a classic of African music, its raw, husky vocals carrying the sadness of colonial war and exile.

## From semba to kizomba

After independence in 1975 and through years of civil war, Angolan musicians kept reinventing their music. In the 1980s, a slower, smoother, romantic style emerged in Luanda, influenced by semba and by the Caribbean zouk then popular across Lusophone Africa: **kizomba**. Kizomba became a global partner dance, while semba itself has been revived by singers such as Paulo Flores.

## Related

Semba's sister song across the Lusophone Atlantic is Cabo Verde's [morna](/music/morna). It shares a guitar-led, Afro-Caribbean history with [Congolese rumba](/music/congolese-rumba), just to the north. For Portuguese-speaking Africa's best-known painter, see [Malangatana](/artists/malangatana).
