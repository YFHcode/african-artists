## Zanzibar and London

Lubaina Himid was born in Zanzibar in 1954. Her father died when she was a baby, and her mother, a textile designer, took her to Britain. She studied theatre design at Wimbledon College of Art and later took a master's degree in cultural history at the Royal College of Art. Theatre and textiles both stayed with her: her work often feels like a stage set, and patterns — especially the printed kanga cloths of East Africa — run through it.

## The Black Arts Movement

In the 1980s Himid was at the centre of the Black Arts Movement in Britain. As well as making her own work, she organised exhibitions that gave Black women artists a place they had been denied, among them *Five Black Women* (1983) and *The Thin Black Line* (1985). Her *A Fashionable Marriage* (1986), a cut-out installation that restaged a Hogarth painting with figures from the politics and art world of Thatcher's Britain, is one of the key works of that period.

## Naming the forgotten

Much of her work looks at the people history left out. *Naming the Money* (2004) is a crowd of about a hundred life-size painted cut-out figures — servants, musicians, cooks, dog-trainers — representing Africans enslaved in eighteenth-century Europe, each given back a name and a story. Other works paint over pages of old newspapers to expose the racism of their images, or show Black women talking, planning and working in bright, flat interiors and landscapes. Her paintings use strong colour and pattern, and often humour, to raise serious questions about who is seen and who is remembered.

## Recognition

Recognition came late and then all at once. In 2017, aged 63, she won the Turner Prize — the first Black woman and the oldest artist to do so. Tate Modern gave her a major exhibition in 2021, and she was appointed CBE. She is a professor emerita at the University of Central Lancashire in Preston, where she lives. In 2026 she represents Britain at the Venice Biennale with *Predicting History: Testing Translation*, in the British Pavilion.
