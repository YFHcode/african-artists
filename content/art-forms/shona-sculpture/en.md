## A name and its problems

Shona is the largest language group in Zimbabwe, and many of the sculptors were Shona. But not all: Tengenenge in particular drew carvers from Malawi, Mozambique, Zambia and Angola as well as Zimbabwe. Critics have also pointed out that "Shona sculpture" makes a modern movement of the 1950s sound like an ancient ethnic tradition. Many writers and artists now prefer **Zimbabwe stone sculpture**. Both names are in use, and this article uses both.

## Ancient stone: the birds of Great Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe does have an ancient tradition of stone carving. At Great Zimbabwe, the stone city that flourished between roughly the eleventh and fifteenth centuries, carvers made soapstone birds mounted on tall columns. Eight were found at the site, and the Zimbabwe Bird is now the national emblem, on the country's flag and coat of arms. The modern movement is not a direct descendant, but it drew strength from that history.

## The National Gallery workshop

The National Gallery of Rhodesia — then the Rhodes National Gallery — opened in Salisbury in 1957 with Frank McEwen, a British art administrator, as its first director. McEwen set up a workshop at the gallery, the Workshop School, where Black artists were given materials, space and encouragement to work from their own imagination rather than from academic models.

Among the first to carve stone was **Joram Mariga** (1927–2000), an agricultural officer often called the father of the movement. Others who came through the workshop or McEwen's circle include **Nicholas Mukomberanwa** (1940–2002), **Henry Munyaradzi** (1931–1998), **Sylvester Mubayi** (born 1942) and **John Takawira** (1938–1989).

McEwen promoted the work tirelessly abroad. In 1971 an exhibition at the Musée Rodin in Paris was enthusiastically received, and shows in London and New York followed.

## Tengenenge

In 1966 Tom Blomefield, who owned a tobacco farm and chrome mine at Tengenenge, north of Harare, found the business ruined by international sanctions against the Rhodesian government. The sculptor Crispen Chakanyuka pointed out that the farm had an outcrop of hard serpentine. Blomefield turned the farm into a sculpture community, where hundreds of artists lived and carved over the following decades. **Bernard Matemera** (1946–2002) was among the best known of them.

## Stones and style

The favoured stone is **serpentine**, in colours from green to black; the hardest, finest black variety is known as **springstone**. Sculptors also use green **verdite**, multicoloured **opal stone**, and purple **lepidolite**. Work is done with hand tools: chisels, files and rasps, then sanding. Finally the stone is heated and waxed, which deepens its colour and brings up a polish, often contrasted with rough, unworked areas.

The style is simplified and rounded: heads, families, mothers and children, animals, and spirits caught in the act of transformation between human and animal forms.

## After independence

Zimbabwe became independent in 1980, and the sculpture became a national pride and a significant export. A second generation includes **Agnes Nyanhongo** (born 1960), one of several women who broke into a field dominated by men, and **Dominic Benhura** (born 1968), known for lively figures of children and families. Large outdoor exhibitions in Europe and North America, and sculpture parks in Harare, kept the work visible. The market also filled with mass-produced pieces, so quality varies widely.
