## From Matalana to the city

Malangatana was born in 1936 in Matalana, a village in southern Mozambique, then a Portuguese colony. He went to mission school, moved to Lourenço Marques — today's Maputo — and worked at a members' tennis club while studying at night and painting.

His professional career began in 1960, when the Portuguese architect Pancho Guedes saw his work, gave him a studio and bought his paintings. Within a year Malangatana had his first solo exhibition.

## What the paintings look like

A Malangatana painting is crowded to its edges: figures with wide eyes and bared teeth, animals, masks and spirits pressed together in strong reds, blues and yellows. The scenes draw on the stories, rituals and fears of village life, on colonial violence and, later, on war. They are disturbing and celebratory at once.

He also wrote poetry, and his words and images share the same intensity.

## Prison

In 1964 the PIDE, Portugal's political police, arrested Malangatana on suspicion of supporting the independence movement. He spent eighteen months in prison, and the work he made around that time forms an important core of his early output.

## Murals and public work

After Mozambique's independence in 1975, Malangatana became a public figure: he painted large murals in Maputo and took part in many cultural and community projects. In September 1997 he painted a fresco during a UNESCO conference on the culture of peace held in Maputo, and the same year UNESCO named him an Artist for Peace. He died in 2011, and his paintings are held in collections in Mozambique, Portugal and beyond.

## Related artists and music

Malangatana and [Esther Mahlangu](/artists/esther-mahlangu) are the best-known southern African artists of their generation to draw directly on local forms. Compare his political imprisonment with that of the Sudanese modernist [Ibrahim El-Salahi](/artists/ibrahim-el-salahi), jailed a decade later.
