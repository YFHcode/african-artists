## Photographing apartheid from the inside

Ernest Cole grew up in a township outside Pretoria and left school early when the apartheid government's Bantu Education system was imposed. He found work in photography in Johannesburg, including at *Drum*, the magazine that gave a generation of Black South African writers and photographers their start, and set himself a single, enormous subject: what apartheid did to the people who lived under it.

He photographed what most of the world never saw. Miners stripped and inspected in groups. Men stopped and arrested under the pass laws. Crowded classrooms, hospital wards, segregated trains and benches, servants in white homes, families in single rooms. He worked carefully and often at risk, and many of the pictures could only have been taken by someone who belonged in the places he photographed.

## House of Bondage

In 1966 Cole left South Africa with his negatives. *House of Bondage*, published in New York in 1967 with his photographs and text, was one of the first books to show apartheid's daily workings to an international audience. It was banned in South Africa, and Cole could not go home.

## Exile

His later years were hard. He lived in the United States and spent time in Sweden, struggled to find work and, by many accounts, at times had no fixed home in New York. He died of cancer in New York on 18 February 1990, a week after Nelson Mandela walked out of prison.

## The lost archive

For decades much of Cole's work was thought to be lost. In 2017 a Swedish bank contacted his family's estate about safety deposit boxes in his name; inside were around 60,000 negatives. The Haitian director Raoul Peck built his documentary *Ernest Cole: Lost and Found* around them, and the film won the Œil d'or, the prize for best documentary, at the 2024 Cannes Film Festival.
