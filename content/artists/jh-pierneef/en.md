J. H. Pierneef was born in Pretoria in 1886 to a family of Dutch origin; his father was a Dutch immigrant. In 1900, during the South African War, the family moved to the Netherlands, where he studied at the Rotterdam Art Academy; he afterwards returned and worked in Pretoria. By the 1920s he had developed his own style: simplified, monumental landscapes built from flat planes, with clear outlines and a strong sense of design, influenced by his studies of art and architecture.

His most famous work is the series of 32 panels commissioned for the new Johannesburg railway station and painted between 1929 and 1932, showing landscapes from across South Africa. They are on long-term loan from the Transnet Foundation to the Rupert Art Foundation, and can be seen at the Rupert Museum in Stellenbosch; South African History Online gives the number of panels as 28. He also worked in woodcut and linocut, and his landscapes became some of the most reproduced images in South Africa. He painted murals for South Africa House in London, completed in 1934.

How to look at a Pierneef: follow the planes, from foreground to horizon, and notice that the clouds, trees and rocks are drawn with the same clear edges, so the land looks built rather than observed. Then ask what is missing. There are almost no people in the pictures, and that absence is the part that readers now question. This reading is the site's own.

Pierneef's ordered landscapes, almost always without people, were embraced by Afrikaner nationalism, and are today also read critically for that vision of an empty land. He died in Pretoria in 1957.

## Where to see the work

The Johannesburg Station Panels are the place to start, at the Rupert Museum in Stellenbosch, and La Motte, in the Cape winelands, publishes a collection page for *Pierneef and Johannesburg*; ask each what is on view. See also [Irma Stern](/artists/irma-stern) and [Vladimir Tretchikoff](/artists/vladimir-tretchikoff), and our list of [South African painters](/artists/country/south-africa/painters).
