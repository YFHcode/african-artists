## A school turned around

Morocco became independent in 1956. In 1962 the painter [Farid Belkahia](/artists/farid-belkahia), aged twenty-eight, became director of the École des Beaux-Arts in Casablanca, which until then had followed a French academic curriculum.

In 1964 he brought in two painters, Mohamed Melehi and Mohamed Chabâa, and two art historians, the Italian Toni Maraini and the Dutch Bert Flint. Together they rewrote the teaching.

## Crafts as modern art

The new curriculum treated Morocco's own visual heritage as a living resource. Students studied and photographed rural carpets, Amazigh (Berber) jewellery and tattoos, painted wooden ceilings, ceramics, calligraphy and architecture — not as folklore to copy, but as a vocabulary of abstraction that was already Moroccan.

The artists' own work followed the same logic. Melehi became known for hard-edged, wave-like abstractions in bright colours. Chabâa worked across painting, graphic design and architectural decoration. Belkahia moved from hammered copper to paintings on stretched leather coloured with henna.

## Art in the square

In May 1969 the group staged *Présence plastique*: open-air exhibitions on the Jemaa el-Fna square in Marrakech and, a few weeks later, on a public square in Casablanca. Paintings stood among street performers, traders and passers-by. The statement was clear: modern art belonged to everyday Moroccan life, not only to galleries.

The artists also designed for print and public space. Melehi created covers for *Souffles*, the influential literary and political magazine founded in 1966, and the group's graphic language spread through posters, books and murals.

## Rediscovery

For decades the school was known mainly in Morocco. Recent exhibitions in Europe, including at Tate St Ives and the Schirn Kunsthalle in Frankfurt, have presented it as one of the major avant-gardes of the post-colonial world.
