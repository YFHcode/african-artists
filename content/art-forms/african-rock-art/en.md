## The oldest images

In 2018 researchers published a small flake of stone from **Blombos Cave**, on South Africa's southern coast, marked with a cross-hatched pattern in red ochre about 73,000 years ago — the oldest known drawing made by a human. The same cave had already yielded pieces of ochre engraved with cross-hatched lines and shell beads of a similar age.

At **Apollo 11 Cave** in southern Namibia — named because it was excavated in 1969, as the Apollo 11 mission returned from the Moon — archaeologists found slabs of stone painted with animals in charcoal and ochre, dated to roughly 25,000 to 30,000 years ago. They are the oldest dated figurative art in Africa.

## The Sahara: when the desert was green

Between roughly 10,000 and 5,000 years ago the Sahara was a land of lakes and grasslands, and the people who lived there left an immense record on its rocks. At **Tassili n'Ajjer** in south-eastern Algeria, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1982, more than 15,000 paintings and engravings survive. Scholars group Saharan rock art into broad periods that follow the changing climate: engravings of large wild animals such as elephants and giraffes; painted human figures with round heads; scenes of cattle herders with their herds; then, as the desert spread, horses and chariots, and finally camels.

The **Tadrart Acacus** in Libya holds similar riches, and in the Gilf Kebir of south-western Egypt the painted figures of the "Cave of Swimmers" became famous through the novel and film *The English Patient*.

## Southern Africa: San paintings

In the **uKhahlamba-Drakensberg** mountains of South Africa and Lesotho, rock shelters hold tens of thousands of paintings by the ancestors of the San, hunter-gatherers who lived there for millennia. The eland, the largest antelope, appears more than any other animal. Many scholars, following the archaeologist David Lewis-Williams, read much of San rock art as connected to the experience of healers in trance — an interpretation that transformed the study of rock art worldwide, though it is still debated.

Other great sites include **Tsodilo Hills** in Botswana, with more than 4,500 paintings, sometimes called "the Louvre of the Desert"; the engravings of **Twyfelfontein** in Namibia; and the **Matobo Hills** in Zimbabwe.

## East Africa and the Horn

The painted shelters of **Kondoa** in Tanzania and the **Chongoni** hills in Malawi — where later paintings are linked to the initiation rites of Chewa farmers — are World Heritage Sites. At **Laas Geel**, in Somaliland, paintings of cattle with decorated necks and of people with raised arms, brought to international attention by French archaeologists in 2002, are among the best preserved in Africa.

## Visiting rock art

Most sites are fragile and best visited with a local guide. Do not touch paintings, and never wet them to make colours stand out for a photograph — a practice once common that damages the pigment. The Trust for African Rock Art (TARA), founded in Nairobi in 1996, works to record and protect sites across the continent.
