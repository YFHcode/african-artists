## Learning to paint walls

Esther Mahlangu was born in 1935 on a farm outside Middelburg, in today's Mpumalanga province. She is Southern Ndebele. In Ndebele communities, women paint the outer walls of homesteads, and girls learn by helping their mothers and grandmothers. Mahlangu began at about ten years old and was an accomplished muralist as a teenager.

Ndebele painting is built from bold geometric shapes — stepped forms, chevrons, triangles, stylised buildings — outlined in thick black lines and filled with flat colour. Older walls used earth pigments; from the mid-twentieth century commercial paints widened the palette. Mahlangu paints freehand, without measuring or masking, and is known for using chicken feathers as brushes.

## Paris, 1989

International attention came in 1989, when Mahlangu was invited to *Magiciens de la Terre* at the Centre Pompidou in Paris, one of the first major exhibitions to show artists from around the world as contemporaries rather than as ethnography. She painted a full-scale house for the show.

## The BMW Art Car

In 1991 BMW commissioned her to paint a 525i for its Art Car series, which had previously been painted by artists such as Alexander Calder, Andy Warhol and Roy Lichtenstein. She was the first woman, and the first artist from Africa, to take part. The car made Ndebele patterns recognisable worldwide; it returned to South Africa for exhibition in 2024, more than thirty years after it was painted.

## From walls to everything

Over the following decades Mahlangu carried the style onto canvas and onto objects of every kind — cars, clothing, homeware — through collaborations with international brands, including later work with BMW and Rolls-Royce. Critics have sometimes questioned the commercial projects; Mahlangu has presented them as a way to keep the tradition visible and to support it financially.

She has also taught. She opened a school at her home to pass Ndebele painting on to younger generations, and her work is held in major collections.

## Why she matters

Mahlangu showed that a tradition practised by rural women could stand in the world's museums without being turned into folklore. Her career runs parallel to that of the Mozambican painter [Malangatana](/artists/malangatana), another southern African artist whose work drew on local forms and reached a global audience. South Africa's best-known recent export in music, [amapiano](/music/amapiano), followed a similar path from local form to global recognition.
