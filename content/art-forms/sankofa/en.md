## What sankofa means

The word is made of three Twi words: **san** (return), **kɔ** (go) and **fa** (fetch or take). Together, *sankɔfa* means "go back and get it".

It comes from an Akan proverb, usually written *Se wo were fi na wosankofa a yenkyi* and translated as **"It is not taboo to go back for what you forgot."** Spellings and translations vary a little from one source to another; the Metropolitan Museum of Art, for example, gives the sense as "There is nothing wrong with learning from hindsight."

The idea is practical, not nostalgic. Sankofa does not say the past was better. It says that what was left behind — knowledge, history, a mistake to be put right — can and should be recovered, so that the way forward is better made.

Sankofa is unusual among adinkra symbols in having two quite different forms with the same name and meaning.

**The sankofa bird.** A bird stands with its feet pointing forward and its head turned back, reaching for an egg on its back or holding one in its beak. The body faces the future; the head looks to the past. According to the Metropolitan Museum, the motif may be based on a mythical bird that flies forward with its head turned backward, or simply on a bird reaching behind itself for food. The egg is often explained as the future, or as the precious thing that must not be left behind.

**The sankofa heart.** The second form is abstract: a heart shape whose two sides curl inward into spirals, usually with two small scrolls at its base. This form is common on adinkra cloth, and in ironwork, jewellery and logos.

Both are correct. Which one people use is a matter of tradition and taste, not of meaning.

## Where the symbol comes from

Sankofa belongs to the visual language of the Akan peoples of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, the same tradition as the stamped [adinkra symbols](/art-forms/adinkra-symbols), in which each sign stands for a proverb or an idea.

The bird is older than the modern fashion for it. Akan metalworkers cast it in brass as a **goldweight**, one of the small weights merchants used to measure gold dust, which served as currency. Museums hold many of them: the Metropolitan Museum of Art has a brass sankofa bird weight dated to the eighteenth or nineteenth century, and the Brooklyn Museum and the Science Museum Group in Britain hold others. The same world of weights, stools and stamped and woven cloth, such as [kente](/art-forms/kente-cloth), carried proverbs into everyday objects across [West Africa](/regions/west-africa).

## Sankofa in the African diaspora

Outside Ghana, sankofa has become the best-known adinkra symbol of all, above all among people of African descent in the Americas, for whom "go back and get it" speaks directly to a history that slavery tried to erase.

- **The film.** *Sankofa* (1993), by the Ethiopian-born director Haile Gerima, follows Mona, a model on a photo shoot at Cape Coast Castle in Ghana, who is carried back in time and lives as Shola, an enslaved woman on a plantation. It was shown in competition at the Berlin Film Festival. When American distributors passed on it, Gerima and his wife, the film-maker Shirikiana Aina, distributed it themselves through their company Mypheduh Films, taking it to independent cinemas in Black communities across the United States. A restored version was released in 2021.
- **Music.** The sankofa symbol features on Janet Jackson's 1997 album *The Velvet Rope*.
- **Schools, community organisations and cultural projects** in many countries carry the name or the sign, usually to say the same thing: know your history.

## Is the heart on the New York coffin a sankofa?

The most famous sankofa in the United States may not be one, and the story is worth knowing.

In 1991, builders working on a federal office building in Lower Manhattan uncovered a cemetery where free and enslaved Africans had been buried from the 1690s to the 1790s. The remains of 419 people were excavated. On the lid of one coffin, known as Burial 101, iron tacks formed a heart-shaped design. Researchers read it as a sankofa, and the symbol became the emblem of the African Burial Ground. The remains were reburied in 2003 in coffins carved by hand in Ghana, and the memorial on the site, designed by Rodney Leon and completed in 2007, uses the sankofa among its symbols.

In 2010 the historian Erik Seeman published a study in the *William and Mary Quarterly* arguing that the design was not a sankofa and probably had no African origin at all; he pointed instead to the hearts used in British and American funeral decoration of the period. The National Park Service, asked about it by the *New York Times*, said only that the design "could be a sankofa symbol" and that "no one knows for sure". Michael Blakey, the scientific director of the original research, was not persuaded by Seeman's argument.

So the honest answer is that nobody can prove what the person who made that coffin intended. What is certain is the modern choice: the descendants and the designers of the memorial took sankofa as its symbol, and that meaning is real whatever the tacks once meant.

## Sankofa in Brazil

Brazil, often described as home to the largest population of African descent outside Africa, has made sankofa its own. The artist, writer and politician **Abdias Nascimento** painted adinkra signs, among them *Sankofa nº 2 – Resgate (Adinkra Asante)* of 1992, and Itaú Cultural's exhibition on his life describes sankofa as the concept that sums up his work. The scholar Elisa Larkin Nascimento has published books under the title *Sankofa* and, with Luiz Carlos Gá, edited the reference book *Adinkra: Sabedoria em Símbolos Africanos*. The University of São Paulo publishes a journal of African history and diaspora studies called *Sankofa*.

## Sankofa tattoos, jewellery and logos

Sankofa is one of the most popular adinkra signs for tattoos and jewellery. Three things are worth knowing before choosing one:

- **Pick the form on purpose.** The bird is easier to recognise; the heart is the form closest to the cloth stamps. They mean the same.
- **Learn the proverb.** The sign is a sentence, not an ornament: *it is not taboo to go back for what you forgot*.
- **Remember whose it is.** Sankofa is part of living Akan culture in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, where adinkra cloth is still worn at funerals and on important occasions.

## Questions

### What does sankofa mean?

"Go back and get it", from the Twi words *san* (return), *kɔ* (go) and *fa* (fetch). It stands for learning from the past.

### What does the sankofa bird symbolise?

A bird that moves forward while looking back: taking what is valuable from the past, shown as an egg, into the future.

### Is sankofa a bird or a heart?

Both. The symbol has two forms, a bird with its head turned back and a stylised heart with spirals, and they carry the same meaning.

### What language is sankofa?

Twi, a language of the Akan people, spoken in Ghana.

### How do you pronounce sankofa?

Roughly *sahn-koh-fah*.

### Is sankofa an adinkra symbol?

Yes. It is one of the adinkra symbols of the Akan, and the best known outside Ghana.
