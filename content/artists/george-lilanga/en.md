George Lilanga was born around 1934 in the Masasi district of southern Tanzania, among the Makonde, a people famous for their wood carving. He grew up carving and making clay figures and, like many Makonde artists, moved to Dar es Salaam, where carving for collectors had become a living. In the early 1970s he joined Nyumba ya Sanaa, the "House of Art" (now the Nyerere Cultural Centre), where artists were trained and their work sold.

His first works were carvings of shetani, the spirit beings that Makonde sculptors had begun to invent in the 1950s (see [Makonde art](/art-forms/makonde-art)). Lilanga turned these spirits into his own world of figures: rubbery bodies with big ears, wide eyes and long fingers, dancing, playing music, gossiping and getting into trouble. He carried them from wood into painting, etching and painted panels, filling the surface with crowds in flat, bright colours. Behind the humour there is often commentary on daily life, money, health and politics in Tanzania.

A 1978 group exhibition in Washington, D.C., in which a large share of the works were his, made his name abroad, and he later showed in Europe, Japan and the United States and in major surveys such as *Africa Remix* (2004). He died in Dar es Salaam in 2005. His humorous, crowded style links him to Tanzania's other popular painting tradition, [Tingatinga](/art-forms/tingatinga).
