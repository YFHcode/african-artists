## A quiet, patient witness

David Goldblatt was born in 1930 in Randfontein, a gold-mining town west of Johannesburg, to a family of Lithuanian Jewish immigrants. He worked in his family's clothing shop before turning to photography full time in the early 1960s.

Unlike the press photographers of his generation, Goldblatt rarely photographed violence. He was interested in the structures that made it possible: the laws, the buildings, the routines and the values of ordinary people. He photographed slowly, usually in black and white, often with a large camera, and wrote detailed captions that are part of the work.

## The books

*On the Mines* (1973), made with the writer Nadine Gordimer, looked at the gold mines on which South Africa's wealth was built and the men who worked in them. *Some Afrikaners Photographed* (1975) portrayed the community that ran the apartheid state, without caricature. *In Boksburg* (1982) studied a white middle-class town in minute detail. *The Transported of KwaNdebele* (1989) followed Black commuters who spent up to eight hours a day on buses between the "homeland" where they were forced to live and their jobs near Pretoria — photographed through the night, on the journey.

Later projects turned to the buildings and monuments of South Africa, and, after 1994, to the democratic country's unfinished business: land, crime and inequality.

## The Market Photo Workshop

In 1989 Goldblatt founded the Market Photo Workshop in Johannesburg to teach photography to people who had been excluded from it, Black South Africans above all. It became one of the most important photography schools in Africa; among its students was [Zanele Muholi](/artists/zanele-muholi).

## Recognition

The Museum of Modern Art in New York gave him a solo exhibition in 1998. He received the Hasselblad Award in 2006 and the Henri Cartier-Bresson Award in 2009. He died in Johannesburg in 2018.
