Romuald Hazoumè was born in Porto-Novo, Benin's capital, in 1962, into a Yoruba family; he grew up between the Catholic faith of his parents and the Vodun traditions around him. Largely self-taught, he began making masks from discarded plastic jerrycans in the mid-1980s. In Benin these containers are everywhere: they carry water, oil and, above all, petrol smuggled across the border from Nigeria on motorbikes piled high with cans.

By cutting, bending and adding a few found objects — wire, combs, cassette tape, feathers — he turns each can into a face with its own personality, often named after people he knows. The masks are funny and expressive, but they also make a point: they answer the Western taste for "authentic" African masks with an object made from the waste of global consumption, and they speak of the dangerous trade in fuel and of how Africa's resources are taken.

His most ambitious work, *La Bouche du Roi* (1997–2005), arranges 304 jerrycan masks in the shape of the *Brookes*, the British slave ship whose diagram became an icon of the abolition campaign, with sounds, smells and objects that recall the Atlantic slave trade and its present-day echoes. The British Museum acquired it in 2007, the bicentenary of the abolition of the British slave trade, and toured it around the UK. The same year he took part in documenta 12 in Kassel.
