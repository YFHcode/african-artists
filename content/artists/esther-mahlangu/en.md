## Learning to paint walls

Esther Mahlangu was born in 1935 on a farm outside Middelburg, in today's Mpumalanga province. She is Southern Ndebele. In Ndebele communities, women paint the outer walls of homesteads, and girls learn by helping their mothers and grandmothers. Mahlangu began at about ten years old, taught by her mother and grandmother, who also taught her beadwork, and she was an accomplished muralist as a teenager.

Ndebele painting is built from bold geometric shapes — stepped forms, chevrons, triangles, stylised buildings — outlined in thick black lines and filled with flat colour. Older walls used earth pigments; from the mid-twentieth century commercial paints widened the palette. Mahlangu paints freehand, without stencils or tape, and is known for using chicken feathers as brushes; the Serpentine Galleries describe her early pigments as natural ones mixed with clay, soil and cow dung.

How to look at a Mahlangu: stand close enough to see the hand. The black lines are drawn freehand, so their small wobbles and changes of pressure are the proof that nothing was measured or masked, and the flat colour between them sits right up to the line. Then step back and read the whole as a pattern that is meant to be seen from across a yard, which is how a wall is seen.

## Paris, 1989

International attention came in 1989, when Mahlangu was invited to *Magiciens de la Terre* at the Centre Pompidou in Paris, one of the first major exhibitions to show artists from around the world as contemporaries rather than as ethnography. Researchers from the French embassy, among them the associate curator André Magnin, had visited South Africa in 1986 and seen the paintings on her home; for the show she was invited to paint a replica of the house at full scale.

## The BMW Art Car

In 1991 BMW commissioned her to paint a 525i for its Art Car series, which had previously been painted by artists such as Alexander Calder, Andy Warhol and Roy Lichtenstein. She was the first woman, and the first artist from Africa, to take part. It was the twelfth car in the series. The car made Ndebele patterns recognisable worldwide; it returned to South Africa for an exhibition at Iziko Museums in Cape Town that ran from 18 February to 11 August 2024, more than thirty years after it was painted.

## From walls to everything

Over the following decades Mahlangu carried the style onto canvas and onto objects of every kind — cars, clothing, homeware — through collaborations with international brands, among them Belvedere vodka, for a campaign against AIDS, Fiat and British Airways, and a BMW 7 Series shown at Frieze in London. Critics have sometimes questioned the commercial projects; Mahlangu has presented them as a way to keep the tradition visible and to support it financially.

She has also taught, passing Ndebele painting on to young people in her village. In 2018 the University of Johannesburg gave her an honorary doctorate, and she holds South Africa's Order of Ikhamanga in silver.

## Recent work

In October 2024 the Serpentine Galleries unveiled her large mural *Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, her first public artwork in the United Kingdom, which stayed on view until March 2026. A solo show, *Time in Color*, opened at Ross + Kramer Gallery in New York in November 2024, and a South African retrospective that year included a documentary about her rural upbringing and Ndebele culture. She lives in Mpumalanga.

## Why she matters

Mahlangu showed that a tradition practised by rural women could stand in the world's museums without being turned into folklore. Her career runs parallel to that of the Mozambican painter [Malangatana](/artists/malangatana), another southern African artist whose work drew on local forms and reached a global audience. The Ndebele are also great [beadworkers](/art-forms/african-beadwork), and South Africa's best-known recent export in music, [amapiano](/music/amapiano), followed a similar path from local form to global recognition.
